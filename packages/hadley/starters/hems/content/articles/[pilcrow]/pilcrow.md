# A short history of the pilcrow

**Every heading on this site carries a small ¶ in its margin. It is older than the printed book.**

Move your pointer to any heading here and a mark appears beside it: ¶. Click it, and the address of the page points to
that very section, ready to be shared. The mark is called a *pilcrow*, and for most of its life it did a very similar
job: it showed where something new begins.

## A line in the margin

The Greeks had no spaces between words and no paragraphs as we know them. What they had was the *paragraphos*,
literally "written beside": a short horizontal stroke in the margin, under the line where a new speaker or a new
thought began. The text ran on without a break; the margin told the reader where to pause.

## From C to ¶

The Romans, and the scribes of the Middle Ages after them, preferred a letter. A new section was a *capitulum*, a
little head, and so they marked it with a C. Over the centuries the C gained a vertical stroke, as many letters in
manuscripts did to set them apart from the text, and then a second one. The bowl was filled in. What remained is the
shape we still use:

:::
¶
:::

The English name is the end of a long chain of mishearings: from *paragraphus* to the Old French *pelagraphe*, and from
there to the Middle English *pylcrafte*, until it settled as *pilcrow*.[^1]

## The rubricator's red

In a medieval workshop, the scribe copied the text in black and left gaps. A second hand, the *rubricator*, went over
the page later and filled them in red: the initials, the headings and the pilcrows. The word comes from *rubrica*,
red ochre, and it survives in every "rubric" we still write.

: scribe

  copied the text, in black ink, and left room for what came later.
: rubricator

  added the red: initials, headings and the pilcrows that opened each section.

## The gap that stayed

Then came printing, and with it more pages than rubricators. Printers kept leaving the space at the start of each
section for a pilcrow to be added by hand, but more and more often nobody added it. The gap remained, empty, and readers
learned to read it.

That is the indent at the start of a paragraph: the place where a red ¶ was meant to go, and never went.

## Hidden, then back

For a long time the pilcrow survived mostly in proofreading, as a correction mark meaning "start a new paragraph
here". Then word processors gave it a second life as the button that shows what is usually hidden: every paragraph
mark in the document, set in a light grey.

On the web it has found a third job. A heading is a place you may want to point someone to, and the ¶ beside it is the
handle. It sits in the margin, as it did two thousand years ago, and it marks the start of something.

[^1]: Keith Houston tells the whole story, and the stories of many other marks, in *Shady Characters: The Secret Life of Punctuation, Symbols, and Other Typographical Marks* (W. W. Norton, 2013).
